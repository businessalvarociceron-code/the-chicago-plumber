Sewer Line Repair in Chicago, IL

Camera-led sewer line repair for backups, odors, roots, and damaged pipe.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

Older Chicago two-flats, brick bungalows, and buildings with original clay or cast-iron pipe can develop trouble in the sewer lateral long before a pipe is visible. Tree roots work through joints, soil can shift around buried pipe, and aging fittings may crack or separate beneath a basement floor, yard, alley, or sidewalk. We inspect sewer line problems across Chicago, Cicero, Stickney, and Oak Park, starting with the fixtures and cleanout that show where wastewater is no longer moving as it should.

A sewer line carries wastewater away from every toilet, tub, shower, sink, and floor drain connected to the building. When the lateral is blocked, damaged, or holding water, the lowest fixture often shows the first backup because gravity sends wastewater there before it rises elsewhere in the system.

A suspected sewer line fault is not work to leave waiting, especially when sewage is backing into a floor drain or basement fixture. A partial blockage can become a full stoppage, and a crack or separated joint can allow wastewater to escape into soil while roots and debris continue to close the pipe.

Sewage odor and gurgling drains

A sewage odor near a basement floor drain, utility room, or cleanout is a common reason people call us. The smell can come from wastewater sitting behind a restriction, a dry floor-drain trap, or a damaged sewer connection, so we look at the drain opening and the line condition instead of assuming the odor has one source.

Gurgling in a toilet when a washing machine drains, bubbling at a tub, or water moving in a nearby floor drain can point to a sewer line that cannot carry air and wastewater together. Those sounds may occur before a complete backup, particularly where roots have narrowed a clay-pipe joint or debris has collected at a low section of pipe.

Other callers report wastewater coming up through a basement floor drain, a toilet that will not flush while other fixtures are used, or a wet area above the buried sewer route. A single slow fixture can be a branch-drain issue, but several fixtures backing up together often directs the inspection toward the building sewer.

These symptoms can look alike from the room where they appear, yet the repair can range from an accessible cleanout issue to a broken underground pipe section. We identify the actual fault before discussing the work because the route, material, and repair scope do not carry the same cost.

Camera inspection and cleanout test

We begin at the cleanout when one is accessible, then check the basement floor drain, toilets, and other fixtures involved in the backup. The cleanout is often a capped pipe near the basement wall or utility area; its opening lets us test the sewer route without removing a toilet or opening a finished wall.

A camera inspection commonly takes about 30 to 60 minutes once the line can be accessed, although a line holding wastewater or packed with roots can require more preparation. We feed the camera through the cleanout, view the interior of the pipe, and check for roots, grease, offset joints, cracks, standing water, collapsed sections, or a foreign object. We also identify the visible pipe material, such as clay, cast iron, or PVC, and measure the likely location of a defect from the access point.

Where the line can be opened and the problem is reachable through the cleanout, we may be able to clear the obstruction and complete the camera check during the same visit. A broken lateral, a section beneath concrete, or pipe that needs excavation is normally planned as separate work so the route, access, replacement material, and any required site preparation can be set out properly.

For a localized damaged section, the repair may involve removing the failed pipe and installing new pipe with properly aligned fittings. When the camera shows repeated root entry or several separated joints, we discuss whether a spot repair addresses the condition or whether a longer section of the sewer route needs attention.

Pipe depth and yard access

The buried pipe route and its depth strongly affect the repair scope. A sewer lateral beneath an open yard is approached differently from one that runs under a concrete basement slab, garage floor, sidewalk, porch, alley, or landscaped area with fences and mature roots.

Pipe age and material also matter. Older clay pipe can fail at individual joints, while aging cast iron may develop internal scale, corrosion, or cracks along more than one section. A camera image helps show whether the repair is confined to one break or whether the line has deterioration beyond the first blockage.

Access at the cleanout can change the amount of investigation needed before repair begins. A seized cleanout cap, a buried cleanout, or a line with no usable access point may require additional work to reach the pipe safely and inspect the full route.

Replacement material availability and the need for a permit, utility marking, or inspection sign-off can also affect scheduling and scope. We explain which portion of the line is visible on the camera, what access is available, and what work must be planned before a buried pipe section is opened.

Cleanout location and pipe material

Before calling, check whether you can locate a sewer cleanout cap in the basement, utility room, crawlspace, yard, or near the building wall. If an exposed pipe section is visible, note whether it appears to be clay, cast iron, PVC, or another material, and whether any cap or fitting is leaking.

Tell us which room has the backup and which fixtures are involved, especially if wastewater is rising through a basement floor drain. If you know when the building was built or when the sewer pipe was last replaced, that age can help us plan the camera route and likely pipe material.
