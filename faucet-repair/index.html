Faucet Repair in Chicago, IL

We fix drips, loose handles, low flow, and leaks around faucets.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

Most people start by tightening a loose handle, wiping mineral scale from the aerator, or turning the under-sink stop valve off and back on. Those steps make sense: a clogged screen or a handle screw can cause weak flow or wobble without a major fault. Yet a drip that returns after the handle is snug, or water that comes from beneath the faucet, usually has a worn seal, cartridge, or connection behind it.

That is where we come in. We work on kitchen sinks, bathroom basins, laundry faucets, and utility taps, tracing the water to the spout, handle stem, supply line, or mounting hardware rather than replacing parts by guesswork. A faucet can leak from the tip while the actual worn piece sits inside the handle, and a wet cabinet can begin with a failed supply connection instead of the faucet body.

A faucet that only drips into the sink can usually be scheduled, but water collecting under the cabinet or running from the handle should not wait. Water at the base can swell cabinet panels, stain flooring, and reach the shutoff valves and supply hoses below the sink.

Dripping spout and loose handle

We hear about a faucet that drips after it is turned off, a handle that spins or feels loose, and a stream that has gone weak while the rest of the house has normal water flow. Other calls involve water coming out around the handle, a spray that shoots sideways from the aerator, or a puddle around the base of the faucet after the sink is used. Hot water only, cold water only, or a leak on both sides also helps narrow down the valve inside the fixture.

A drip at the spout can point to a cartridge, washer, or valve seat, while a leak around the handle may be an O-ring or packing seal. Low flow may be mineral buildup in the aerator, debris in the supply path, or a restriction inside the cartridge. These symptoms can look similar from the counter, but the failed part, access to it, and work involved are not necessarily the same.

Faucet inspection visit

We start by identifying the faucet make, handle style, and the room fixture, then test the hot and cold sides separately. We check the spout, aerator, handle movement, base, supply hoses, shutoff valves, and the underside of the sink cabinet for moisture. When needed, we time the water flow for sixty seconds and check pressure at a suitable threaded connection; residential plumbing pressure is generally kept below eighty psi.

We isolate the water, remove the handle and trim, and inspect the cartridge, O-rings, washers, retaining clip, and valve body for wear or mineral buildup. A straightforward aerator, cartridge, seal, or supply-line repair often takes thirty to sixty minutes once the water is shut off and the matching part is available. We can usually complete accessible repairs during the visit, while a discontinued cartridge, specialty finish, replacement faucet, or concealed valve issue may need a separate booking after the correct part is located.

Cartridge age and seized shutoffs

The age and design of the cartridge matter because older faucet lines can use parts that are no longer stocked locally, while some current fixtures use brand-specific stems, clips, and seals. A single-handle faucet with an accessible cartridge is different from a wall-mounted fixture or a faucet with its valve body hidden behind tile. Corrosion around the handle, mounting nut, or retaining clip can also add disassembly work before the worn part can be reached.

Access below the sink is another factor. A shutoff valve that turns freely allows us to isolate the fixture cleanly, but a seized or leaking stop valve may need attention before faucet work can proceed. A part that must be ordered, a building water shutoff, or approval from a property manager can also affect the scope, especially in a condo or multi-unit building.

Model numbers and faucet location

Before calling, note which room has the faucet and whether it is the kitchen sink, bathroom basin, laundry sink, or utility tap. If you can, look for the brand name on the spout or handle and the model number on the original paperwork, installation sheet, or underside of the fixture trim. Let us know whether the drip is running now, whether it is hot, cold, or both, and whether water is appearing inside the cabinet. A clear photo of the handle, spout, and space around the shutoff valves can help us identify the cartridge and plan access.
