Leak Repair in Chicago, IL

We trace and repair pipe, ceiling, cabinet, and wall leaks across Chicago.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

A puddle spreading from a kitchen cabinet and a brown ring appearing on a hallway ceiling both get called a leak, but they are not the same repair. From the hallway, a floor puddle usually points toward a fixture connection, supply tube, shutoff valve, or drain nearby. A ceiling stain can be caused by a pipe above, a shower connection, or water that has traveled along framing from another room.

We trace both visible water and hidden moisture. From 210 N Racine Ave, we cover leak repairs in Chicago, Cicero, Stickney, and Oak Park, checking the fixture, pipe route, cabinet base, ceiling area, and nearby valves before opening a wall or replacing a fitting.

An active drip, wet drywall, growing ceiling stain, or water reaching a cabinet should not wait because water can move through insulation, framing, and flooring after the original leak starts. A dry stain that has not changed can be scheduled for inspection, but we still need to determine whether the pipe or connection behind it remains wet.

Water under the sink

We hear calls about water under the sink, a drip through the ceiling, a hissing sound in the wall, a wet spot beside the toilet, and a cabinet floor that keeps getting damp. Water that appears only while a fixture runs can point to a drain joint, overflow, trap, or discharge connection. Water that collects while every fixture is off can point instead to a pressurized supply line, angle stop, valve body, or appliance connection.

A stain on a ceiling may be old water, condensation, or an active pipe leak, while a damp cabinet can come from a loose compression nut, a split supply tube, or water running down from the countertop. These symptoms can look alike from the room, but the failed part, access needed, and repair scope can be very different once we inspect the pipe and fixture.

Leak inspection and test

At the callout, we first identify where water is appearing and check whether the source is above, behind, or below that spot. We inspect supply lines, shutoff valves, trap joints, pipe fittings, fixture bases, access panels, and visible framing. A moisture meter helps us compare the wet area with surrounding drywall or cabinet material, while a pressure check and water-meter observation can help distinguish a pressurized pipe leak from water released only when a fixture is used.

Initial tracing commonly takes between thirty and sixty minutes when the pipe and connections are accessible. We can usually replace an exposed supply tube, compression fitting, washer, shutoff valve, or damaged section of accessible pipe during the same visit when the required material is available. If the source sits behind tile, inside a finished ceiling, below a slab, or in a specialty valve that needs an ordered part, we isolate the leak where possible and schedule the opening, replacement, and wall-access work separately.

Shutoff valves, pipe age, wall access

The condition of the shutoff valve, the age and material of the pipe, and the route to the wet area all affect the scope of a leak repair. A recent leak at an exposed braided supply line or cabinet connection is generally more direct to reach than a pinhole in aging copper behind a tiled shower wall. Corroded threaded fittings, brittle plastic connectors, and previous patchwork can require more disassembly before we can install a sound replacement.

A shutoff valve that will not close can add work because the water supply must be controlled before a fitting is removed. Access through an existing panel, unfinished basement ceiling, or open cabinet keeps the repair focused on the pipe; finished drywall, plaster, tile, or built-in cabinetry can require careful opening and later restoration. Specialty cartridges, uncommon valve bodies, and parts that must be ordered extend the job beyond the initial visit, and some building work may require a sign-off after the plumbing repair.

Fixture model and access panel

Before calling, note the room, the fixture, and the exact place where water is showing, such as under a vanity, below a ceiling light, beside a toilet base, or at a pipe in the basement. Tell us whether water appears while the fixture is running, after it is shut off, or when no fixture has been used. If the leaking item is a specialty valve, filter housing, or faucet, the make and model from a label on the valve body, cabinet paperwork, or installation booklet can help us identify the correct part. Let us know the approximate age of the fitting and whether there is an access panel, open cabinet, basement ceiling, or finished wall around the pipe.
