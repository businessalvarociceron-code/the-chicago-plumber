Toilet Repair in Chicago, IL

Running, leaking, or weak-flushing toilet repairs across Chicago.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

A toilet that runs for a minute after flushing may seem like a small tank problem. After a week, the fill valve can cycle repeatedly and send water through the overflow tube. After a month, a worn flapper or misadjusted chain can keep the tank refilling long enough to show up on the water bill.

A slow flush can also change from an occasional inconvenience to a bowl that will not clear paper or waste. Repeated partial flushes leave debris in the trapway, while water around the base can soak the flooring, loosen the toilet bolts, and damage the wax ring beneath the bowl.

We repair toilet tanks, bowls, fill valves, flush valves, flappers, handles, supply lines, and base seals throughout Chicago, Cicero, Stickney, and Oak Park. A handle that needs a minor adjustment may wait briefly, but a running tank, leaking base, or bowl that threatens to overflow should not wait because water can reach the floor, subfloor, and adjacent room.

Running water and weak flush

“My toilet keeps running” often means water is passing a flapper at the bottom of the tank, although a fill valve that will not shut off can create the same sound. You may hear the tank refill every few minutes, see water entering the overflow tube, or notice ripples in the bowl when nobody has used the fixture.

“The toilet will not flush” can describe a loose trip lever, a broken lift chain, a handle that has stopped catching, or a tank with too little water for a full flush. A weak flush, a bowl that rises before draining, and a toilet that needs two or three flushes are different symptoms, even when they occur at the same fixture.

Water on the bathroom floor may come from the supply connection, tank bolts, a cracked tank, condensation, or the seal below the bowl. A sewer-like odor around the base, a rocking bowl, and stains on the ceiling below also point to different parts of the toilet assembly.

These faults can look similar from outside the tank, but the failed part, the labor involved, and the materials needed are not the same. We inspect the bowl, tank, shutoff valve, and floor connection before identifying the repair.

Tank and bowl inspection

At a toilet inspection, we start by flushing the fixture and watching the tank fill, the flapper seat, the overflow tube, and the bowl water level. We check the handle, lift chain, fill valve, supply line, tank bolts, and shutoff valve for movement, corrosion, or active water. A tank check and adjustment commonly takes about 20 to 40 minutes when the issue is visible and the fixture is accessible.

We can test the water pressure at the nearby supply and check whether the stop valve fully closes before removing a fill valve or supply line. Residential plumbing pressure is commonly kept in the range of 40 to 60 psi; unusually high pressure can shorten the life of rubber seals and fill-valve components. We also check for water at the base and test whether the bowl shifts against the floor.

Flappers, fill valves, handles, chains, and standard supply connectors are often replaced during the same visit when the correct part is available. Common flush valves are sized around 2 or 3 inches, and the tank model determines which seal, gasket, and hardware fit. A cracked bowl, damaged flange, seized shutoff valve, concealed floor damage, or a special-order tank part may require a return visit after the needed material or approval is arranged.

Tank age and shutoff valve

The age and make of the toilet tank affect the repair because older flush valves, proprietary flappers, discontinued handles, and corroded tank bolts may not match current replacement parts. A common fill valve or flapper is usually a more direct repair than a specialty cartridge or a part that must be ordered for a particular tank model.

Access at the shutoff valve and around the bowl also changes the work involved. A toilet in a tight water closet, a valve behind cabinetry, or a supply line blocked by a pedestal sink can take more setup and testing than an open bathroom fixture. If the shutoff valve has seized, leaks when turned, or will not fully stop the water, that valve may need attention before the tank component can be changed.

A bowl that rocks can point to loose closet bolts, a failing wax ring, uneven tile, or trouble at the floor flange. Resetting a bowl requires removing it from the flange and checking the condition of the seal and floor surface, rather than simply tightening a bolt.

Some buildings require management access, water shutoff coordination, or sign-off before work at a shared plumbing connection can proceed. We explain the condition of the tank, bowl, valve, and floor connection so you can see which part of the repair is driving the scope.

Tank model and bowl location

Before calling, look inside the tank lid or along the back wall of the tank for the manufacturer name and model number; many toilets print or stamp this information on the porcelain. Tell us which bathroom holds the fixture, such as the first-floor powder room or an upstairs hall bath, especially when the toilet is above a finished ceiling.

If water is running now, note whether it enters the overflow tube, leaks from the tank, or appears around the bowl base after a flush. Let us know if the handle is loose, the bowl is clogged, or the toilet rocks on the tile, since those symptoms point to different parts and tools.

A photo of the tank interior, fill valve, flapper, and shutoff valve can help identify the likely fitting before the visit. Mention any cabinet, pedestal sink, or restricted space beside the bowl that affects access to the supply line or closet bolts.
