Water Heater Repair in Chicago, IL

Water heater diagnosis and repair for tanks that leak, run cold, or make noise.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

Two calls often get described as a broken water heater, but they are not the same job. A dry utility-room floor with cold water at the shower or kitchen faucet points us toward a heating, control, burner, or element fault. A puddle beside the tank, a steady drip from the relief valve pipe, or water staining the floor near the heater points us toward a leak or pressure problem instead.

We handle both situations across Chicago, Cicero, Stickney, and Oak Park. On a gas unit, we check the pilot, burner, vent connection, gas control, and temperature setting. On an electric tank, we check the disconnect, reset, thermostats, heating elements, wiring terminals, and tank connections before deciding which part has failed.

A tank that is only producing lukewarm water may allow time for a planned repair, provided there is no leak, smoke, burning odor, or electrical issue. Water on the floor, discharge from the temperature-and-pressure relief valve, a gas odor, or a hot electrical cover should not wait, because a failing tank, valve, burner, or wire can damage the surrounding room or create a safety issue.

No hot water or water on floor

When you call about no hot water, we ask whether every faucet is cold or whether the water starts hot and turns cold after a few minutes. A short burst of hot water can come from a failed lower heating element, a thermostat problem, a broken dip tube, or heavy sediment taking up space inside the tank.

Water on the floor may be a loose supply connector, a leaking drain valve, a relief-valve discharge pipe, condensation around the flue, or corrosion through the tank jacket. A popping or rumbling sound during a heating cycle often comes from mineral sediment at the bottom of the tank, while a pilot that will not stay lit points us toward the thermocouple, pilot assembly, gas control, venting, or combustion air.

Other calls involve rusty hot water, a breaker that trips when the heater runs, a burner that lights and then shuts down, or a temperature that is far hotter than the faucet setting suggests. Those symptoms can sound alike from the kitchen or hallway, but we separate the tank, control, valve, electrical, and burner faults because each repair uses different parts and labor.

Tank and burner inspection

During an inspection, we first identify the fuel type, tank size, model label, visible leaks, and shutoff locations. We inspect the cold-water inlet, hot-water outlet, drain valve, relief valve, dielectric fittings, expansion tank where present, and the floor around the base of the heater. On a gas model, we inspect the pilot flame, burner compartment, vent pipe, draft hood, and gas control; on an electric model, we isolate power before checking the reset, wire connections, thermostats, and elements.

We can measure outlet water temperature at a nearby faucet and compare the heater setting with the water leaving the tank. Many homes use a stored-water setting around 120°F to limit scalding, although the correct setting depends on the household and equipment. We may test voltage and element continuity on an electric heater, or use a manometer when a gas-pressure check is needed as part of the diagnosis. A straightforward inspection commonly takes 30 to 60 minutes, while a tank with active leakage, buried access, or multiple failed components takes longer to trace safely.

When the matching part is available, we can often replace items such as a thermostat, heating element, thermocouple, pilot component, relief valve, or supply connector during the same visit. A corroded tank, a specialty control that must be ordered, major vent changes, or a full tank replacement is generally scheduled as separate work because the old unit, water connections, venting, and installation space all need to be addressed together.

Anode rods, shutoffs, and access

The condition and age of the tank matter because a worn internal anode rod, a rusted drain valve, or corrosion at the water connections can make a small symptom part of a larger tank problem. A newer heater with a failed thermostat or pilot component is often a different repair from an older tank with rust beneath the jacket or moisture around the base.

Access also changes the scope of the work. A heater in an open basement utility area gives us room for a drain hose, meter, hand tools, and replacement part, while a tank in a tight closet, finished enclosure, or low-clearance alcove can require extra setup. Clearance above the tank is especially important when an anode rod needs to be removed.

A seized water shutoff can add work because the supply must be controlled before certain valves or connections can be replaced. If the required control, burner assembly, or manufacturer-specific part is no longer stocked and has to be ordered, the repair may need a return visit. We also account for permit or inspection sign-off when replacement, venting, or fuel connections require it for the property.

Model plate and tank age

Before you call, look for the model and serial number on the rating plate attached to the side of the tank jacket, often near the gas control or electrical access panel. We also need the approximate age of the heater, whether it is gas or electric, and the room where the tank is installed. Tell us whether water is actively running from the relief pipe, drain valve, top connections, or base of the tank. If you can safely see the access route, let us know about basement stairs, a utility closet, low ceiling clearance, or an enclosure around the heater. Call us at (847) 809-2341 with the plate information, since an active tank leak can spread across the floor and damage the tank base, wall, and nearby materials while it waits.
