Sump Pump Repair in Chicago, IL

Sump pump repairs for pits, floats, check valves, and discharge lines.

Call (847) 809-2341 Ask a question

Older Chicago homes with finished basements, laundry rooms below grade, or utility pits often rely on a sump pump that has been sitting idle between heavy rain events. The pump, float switch, check valve, and discharge pipe can all age without obvious warning, especially where the pit collects silt, gravel, or lint from nearby floor drains.

We work on sump pump setups across Chicago, Cicero, Stickney, and Oak Park, including pedestal pumps, submersible pumps, battery backup systems, and pits with exterior discharge lines. A pump may look normal until the water level reaches the float, when a seized impeller, failed switch, blocked outlet, or loose plug becomes clear.

A sump pump fault should not wait when water is rising in the pit, the basin is overflowing, or the pump is cycling without clearing water. Unlike a fixture drip, a failed pump can leave basement flooring, stored boxes, drywall, and appliances exposed to water during the next storm or snowmelt.

Pump humming, pit filling

Customers often call us because the sump pump is humming but no water is leaving the pit. That sound can point to an impeller jammed by debris, a worn motor, or a discharge line that is blocked or frozen outside.

A pit that fills up while the pump stays silent can mean the float is stuck against the basin wall, the switch has failed, the outlet has lost power, or the GFCI receptacle has tripped. We also hear about pumps that run every few minutes after rain has stopped, which can come from a leaking check valve that lets water fall back into the pit.

Other signs include a vibrating discharge pipe, water returning through the outlet, a burning odor near the motor, or a pump that only starts when the float is lifted by hand. These symptoms can look alike from the basement floor, but the pump, switch, valve, and discharge line are separate faults with different repair work and different parts involved.

Sump pit inspection

On a sump pump callout, we first check the pit water level, pump position, float travel, power cord, receptacle, and the condition of the basin cover. We test whether the float starts the pump and watch whether the water level drops rather than returning through the discharge pipe.

We inspect the check valve for an arrow pointing in the direction of flow, listen for reverse flow after the pump stops, and check visible discharge fittings for cracks or loose couplings. A typical functional check can take 15 to 30 minutes, while clearing a packed pit or tracing a concealed discharge route takes longer. We also confirm that the pump is properly supported above loose sediment so debris is less likely to enter the intake.

When the fault is a stuck float, failed check valve, loose connection, blocked intake, or accessible pump, we can often complete the repair during the same visit if the needed part is available. A replacement pump, a battery backup component, extensive discharge piping, or work requiring access beyond the utility room may need a follow-up visit after we identify the correct model and route.

Pump age and discharge access

The age and condition of the pump affect the repair decision because a worn motor, damaged housing, or obsolete switch may make a replacement part impractical. The model plate on the motor helps us match the pump capacity, outlet size, cord arrangement, and float style instead of fitting a part that does not suit the pit.

Access to the sump basin and discharge pipe also changes the scope of work. A clear utility room with an exposed check valve is different from a pit behind stored shelving, a sealed cover, or a discharge line routed through finished walls, crawlspace areas, or an exterior wall.

A seized shutoff, corroded threaded fitting, cracked PVC coupling, or valve glued into an older discharge assembly can add work because the affected section may need to be cut out and rebuilt. If a specific pump, backup unit, lid, or fitting has to be ordered, the repair is shaped by that part and by whether the system can safely remain out of service while it is obtained.

Pump label and pit access

Before calling, check the model number and make on the pump label, usually printed on the motor housing or under the basin cover. Let us know whether the pit is in a basement utility room, laundry area, crawlspace, or another lower-level room, and whether water is currently rising in the basin. If you can see the discharge pipe, note whether water is coming back into the pit after the pump stops. Clear boxes, shelving, and stored items away from the pit cover so we can reach the float, plug, and check valve.
